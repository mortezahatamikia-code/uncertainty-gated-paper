\begin{abstract}
Diagnosing foliar plant diseases early and reliably is central to precision agriculture and robotic crop scouting. Bringing vision foundation models to agricultural edge devices, however, runs into two lasting obstacles: very few labeled examples are available for novel pathogens, and the hardware is tightly constrained. We present \textbf{BioDINO-Edge}, an adaptive dual-foundation framework for accurate few-shot plant pathology on resource-constrained hardware. It combines two complementary foundation models: a lightweight self-supervised patch extractor (\textbf{DINOv2 Small}, 22.1M parameters, 21.1\,ms on CPU) and an organismal vision-language model (\textbf{BioCLIP ViT-B/16}) that captures fine-grained pathological hierarchies. To separate intra-species necrotic lesions that look alike, without leaking semantics between classes, we introduce \textbf{Purified Taxonomic Domain Synthesis} and \textbf{Regularized Fisher-Discriminant Alignment}, which applies analytical Ledoit-Wolf covariance shrinkage over the joint 896-dimensional manifold. A \textbf{Soft-Anchored Transductive Self-Calibration} step then removes the prototype shift inherent to 5-shot learning without any backpropagation. At inference time, an adaptive Shannon-entropy gate routes each query: confident ones follow the fast edge path, while ambiguous lesions are sent to a deeper consensus path. We package the system as a \textbf{Dual-Tier Edge Architecture}: a lightweight standalone INT8 model (22.05\,MB working set, 15.13\,MB losslessly compressed payload, 22.73\,MB complete native APK, $>$45 FPS on an edge CPU), complemented by an optional 83.6\,MB on-demand companion model. On the expanded PlantVillage 5-shot benchmark covering 11 novel intra-species classes (Apple, Grape, Potato; 9,330 test images), BioDINO-Edge reaches a \textbf{98.72\% peak accuracy}, a \textbf{98.41\% peak F1-score}, and a 30-episode mean of \textbf{97.37\% $\pm$ 0.73\%} (95\% CI: [97.11\%, 97.63\%]). Cross-species confusion is held to \textbf{0.011\%}, and the false-negative disease rate to \textbf{0.604\%}. On the unconstrained, in-the-wild PlantDoc benchmark (27 classes), the model attains an episodic mean of \textbf{83.61\% $\pm$ 11.69\%} (individual episodes reaching \textbf{100.00\%} and a 95\% confidence ceiling of \textbf{86.85\%}), which is \textbf{13.00} points above the most recent multimodal state of the art from 2026.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Plant pests and diseases destroy an estimated 20\%--40\% of crops annually \cite{richard2022control}. Traditional diagnosis of plant pathogens heavily relies on visual leaf characteristics such as color, shape, and lesions \cite{khakimov2022traditional}. In field practice, visual inspection is slow, depends on trained scorers, and becomes ambiguous when different pathogens produce similar lesions \cite{shoaib2023advanced}; faster and more reproducible identification is therefore needed.

Deep learning provides an automated alternative for plant disease identification by learning discriminative features directly from images \cite{shoaib2023advanced}. However, conventional supervised models require large labeled datasets to achieve reliable diagnosis. Such datasets are difficult to acquire in real-world agricultural settings due to the limited availability and diversity of field images \cite{shoaib2023advanced, shafik2023systematic}.

FSL has become a common workaround: instead of collecting thousands of labeled images, it trains a model to recognize new disease classes from a handful of support examples. Recent research has attempted to apply FSL using cross-domain few-shot learning models---such as CDFSL-BDC \cite{yang2024laboratory} and MAFDE-DN4 \cite{zhao2024mafde}---as well as few-shot metric domain adaptation (FMDA) \cite{kudo2025few} and visual-semantic fusion (TSMF) \cite{zhou2026vsf}, alongside recent edge-federated approaches \cite{sharif2026real}. However, these approaches exhibit several practical limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Domain Shift \& Target-Domain Dependency:} Cross-domain models (such as CDFSL-BDC \cite{yang2024laboratory} and MAFDE-DN4 \cite{zhao2024mafde}) perform poorly under cross-domain evaluation due to complex backgrounds and multi-scale lesions, while metric adaptation methods (such as FMDA \cite{kudo2025few}) still require labeled samples from the target domain.
    \item \textbf{Semantic Misalignment:} Multimodal models relying on LLM text descriptions (e.g., GPT-4o in TSMF \cite{zhou2026vsf}) fail to generalize well to real-world examples, suffering from semantic inaccuracies and a lack of fine-grained alignment.
    \item \textbf{Deployment Inefficiencies:} Federated and edge models (e.g., \cite{sharif2026real}) lack sufficient testing using real-world data streams, making them inefficient for real-time edge deployment.
\end{itemize}

We introduce BioDINO-Edge, a dual-foundation pipeline tailored to cross-domain few-shot plant pathology under edge-hardware constraints. It combines visual patch tokens from DINOv2 \cite{oquab2023dinov2} with clean botanical domain prompts from BioCLIP \cite{stevens2024bioclip}, eliminating the need to fine-tune foundation models to detect subtle necrotic lesions. Using closed-form Fisher-LDA \cite{fisher1936use} and Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage \cite{ledoit2004well}, our model completely removes backpropagation during adaptation, enabling rapid on-device calibration in milliseconds.

The key contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dual-Foundation Foliar Representation:} Uniting self-supervised visual patch tokens from DINOv2 with purified botanical domain prompts from BioCLIP to resolve subtle necrotic lesions without foundation-model fine-tuning.
    \item \textbf{Closed-Form Analytical Adaptation:} Integrating Fisher-LDA with Ledoit--Wolf covariance shrinkage and soft-anchored transductive recalibration to overcome high-dimensionality issues and 5-shot centroid shifts within 25 milliseconds without backpropagation.
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Two-Tier Edge Deployment:} Routing queries through a Shannon \cite{shannon1948mathematical} margin-entropy gate to execute an ultra-lightweight 22.73\,MB standalone APK alongside an 83.6\,MB on-demand module within a strict 110\,MB memory footprint.
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Cross-Domain Validation:} Under the standard 5-way 5-shot protocol, BioDINO-Edge outperforms the multimodal VSF baseline by 13.00 percentage points on PlantDoc and by 6.86 percentage points on PlantVillage, while remaining within edge-device memory budgets.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proposed BioDINO-Edge Architecture}
\label{sec:methodology}

\textbf{Problem Formulation and Mathematical Setting.} We formulate cross-domain few-shot foliar pathology classification as follows. Let $\mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$ denote the RGB image domain, and let $\mathcal{Y} = \{1, \dots, C\}$ denote the label space of $C$ novel phytopathological categories. During adaptation, the model receives a small support set $\mathcal{S} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_s}$ containing exactly $K$ labeled leaf exemplars per category, such that $N_s = C \times K$ (under standard 5-shot protocols, $K=5$). Given an unlabelled query leaf $\mathbf{x}_q \in \mathcal{Q}$, the goal is to predict its true phytopathological designation $y_q \in \mathcal{Y}$.

Solving this task under real-world conditions presents two fundamental mathematical and representation challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Vegetative Subspace Confounding:} In plant pathology, diagnostic symptoms reside in localized, high-frequency necrotic specks, chlorotic halos, or fungal lesions. However, raw representations extracted by general-purpose vision backbones are predominantly dominated by shared, non-pathological botanical factors---such as chlorophyll pigmentation, leaf venation, specular cuticle reflection, and ambient field illumination. In standard Euclidean feature spaces, this shared vegetative variance often exceeds the subtle inter-class variance separating distinct pathogen infections, misguiding nearest-centroid classifiers.
    \item \textbf{Small-Sample Rank Deficiency ($N \ll d$):} While multi-scale foundation models provide expressive representations in dimension $d$ (here $d = 896$), estimating empirical class covariance from a handful of support leaves yields a severely singular within-class scatter matrix with $\text{rank}(\mathbf{S}_W) \le N - C \ll d$. Iterative gradient fine-tuning on such limited data leads to immediate overfitting and catastrophic representation drift, whereas traditional unregularized Fisher projections are ill-posed due to non-invertibility.
\end{enumerate}

To resolve these challenges from first principles, BioDINO-Edge is formulated as an analytical, gradient-free representation learning framework (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}). The framework decouples the learning process into: (i) constructing a dual-foundation foliar manifold spanning localized spatial lesion geometry and organismal phylogenetic priors; (ii) resolving the small-sample covariance singularity via analytical Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage to obtain optimal closed-form Fisher discriminant projections; and (iii) mitigating prototype shift through soft-anchored transductive recalibration. Crucially, because the entire mathematical adaptation reduces to closed-form algebraic solutions requiring zero backpropagation and sub-millisecond inference scoring ($O(Cd)$ FLOPs), it naturally unlocks an adaptive, uncertainty-gated dual-tier execution pipeline tailored for resource-constrained edge hardware.

\subsection{Dual-Foundation Foliar Representation Space}
\label{sec:dual_backbone}

A reliable diagnosis of foliar pathology requires simultaneous access to two complementary visual dimensions: (i) localized spatial lesion geometry (e.g., fungal pustules, necrotic margins, and foliar blight borders), and (ii) organismal and phylogenetic context (e.g., host plant genus, leaf morphology, and evolutionary susceptibility). Standard vision backbones typically prioritize one scale at the expense of the other; generic vision models miss subtle histological patterns, while specialized botanical classifiers often fail to isolate high-frequency spatial lesions.

We reconcile these two scales by formulating a joint representation space $\mathcal{Z} \subset \mathbb{R}^{896}$ spanned by two parallel, frozen foundation encoders:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Lesion Geometry ($f_{\text{dino}}$):} To isolate localized foliar lesions without requiring manual pixel-level annotations, we employ DINOv2 Small (22.1M parameters, patch size $14 \times 14$) \cite{oquab2023dinov2}. Pre-trained via self-supervised masked image modeling, DINOv2 develops emergent spatial attention that cleanly segments chlorotic margins and necrotic specks from healthy vegetative tissue. For any input image $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}$, we extract and $\ell_2$-normalize the global [CLS] token:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f}_{\text{dino}} = \frac{f_{\text{dino}}(\mathbf{x})}{\|f_{\text{dino}}(\mathbf{x})\|_2} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Taxonomic and Phylogenetic Grounding ($f_{\text{bio}}$):} To incorporate evolutionary and histological domain priors, we integrate the vision transformer of BioCLIP ViT-B/16 (86.6M parameters) \cite{stevens2024bioclip}. Having been trained on over 10 million organismal images across the Tree of Life, BioCLIP embeds deep botanical taxonomy and cellular characteristics. Its normalized visual representation is:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f}_{\text{bio}} = \frac{f_{\text{bio}}(\mathbf{x})}{\|f_{\text{bio}}(\mathbf{x})\|_2} \in \mathbb{R}^{512}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Joint Foliar Manifold ($\mathbf{z}$):} Concatenating both embeddings constructs a unified 896-dimensional representation:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z} = \left[\mathbf{f}_{\text{bio}} \,\|\, \mathbf{f}_{\text{dino}}\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{896}
    \end{equation}
    which simultaneously preserves high-frequency lesion geometry and broad botanical context within a unified metric space.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Taxonomic Multi-Prompt Domain Synthesis}
\label{sec:taxonomic_prompts}

In multimodal vision-language architectures, prompt design dictates the orientation of zero-shot semantic prototypes. Conventional templates such as ``a photo of a leaf'' \cite{radford2021clip, zhou2022coop} are inadequate for plant pathology; they lack the descriptive specificity required to separate distinct fungal pathogens that infect the same host plant. To ground the semantic space in verified phytopathology, we synthesize an ensemble $\{T_c^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^M$ of scientific prompts for each class $c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$:
\begin{equation}
T_c^{(m)} = \text{Prompt}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\text{latin}}^{(c)}, \mathcal{D}^{(c)}, \mathcal{P}^{(c)}, \mathcal{M}_m^{(c)}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}_{\text{latin}}^{(c)}$ specifies the host plant binomial (\textit{Malus domestica} for apple, \textit{Vitis vinifera} for grape, \textit{Solanum tuberosum} for potato); $\mathcal{D}^{(c)}$ designates the pathology; $\mathcal{P}^{(c)}$ cites the causal fungal or bacterial agent (e.g., \textit{Venturia inaequalis}, \textit{Guignardia bidwellii}, \textit{Phytophthora infestans}); and $\mathcal{M}_m^{(c)}$ details macroscopic symptom morphology---from dark olive velvety spots to concentric target-board necrosis.

\textbf{Purified Botanical Formulation for Healthy Controls.} Multimodal transformers do not inherently model syntactic negation in the same manner as human observers. Describing a healthy leaf using conventional negative descriptors (e.g., ``clean foliage without fungal pustules or necrotic lesions'') frequently causes cross-modal attention to attend strongly to the salient pathogen tokens (``fungal pustules'', ``necrotic lesions''). This induces severe semantic bleed, drawing the healthy class centroid toward diseased clusters. We resolve this by strictly enforcing affirmative morphological phrasing for all healthy control classes (e.g., ``clean lamina, intact smooth leaf blade, vibrant photosynthetic mesophyll''). This guarantees a well-separated semantic anchor for healthy foliage.

Projecting these botanical prompts through the frozen BioCLIP text encoder $\phi_{\text{bio}}(\cdot)$ yields the linguistic prototypes:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_{\text{text}}^{(c)} = \text{Normalize}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M \phi_{\text{bio}}(T_c^{(m)})\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{512}
\end{equation}

To capture intra-class visual variations under strict 5-shot constraints, we apply an 8-fold multi-view orbital augmentation policy $\mathcal{T}_{\text{aug}}(\cdot)$ (affine rotations, color perturbations, and spatial reflections). This expands the raw $K=5$ support leaves into $|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c| = 40$ augmented samples per class ($N = 440$ total samples across $C = 11$ classes). The visual support prototypes are computed via empirical averaging:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_{\text{dino}}^{(c)} = \text{Normalize}\left(\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c|}\sum_{\tilde{x} \in \tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c} f_{\text{dino}}(\tilde{x})\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{384}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_{\text{bio, vis}}^{(c)} = \text{Normalize}\left(\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c|}\sum_{\tilde{x} \in \tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c} f_{\text{bio}}(\tilde{x})\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{512}
\end{equation}
Finally, we fuse visual and linguistic representations into a multimodal consensus prototype ($\lambda = 0.20$):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_{\text{bio}}^{(c)} = \text{Normalize}\left((1-\lambda)\mathbf{P}_{\text{bio, vis}}^{(c)} + \lambda \mathbf{P}_{\text{text}}^{(c)}\right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Analytical Fisher Alignment via Ledoit-Wolf Covariance Shrinkage}
\label{sec:fisher_alignment}

Metric few-shot learners such as Prototypical Networks \cite{snell2017prototypical} assign class probabilities based on Euclidean distances to class centroids, implicitly assuming an isotropic, spherical covariance structure ($\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{I}$). In foliar pathology, this assumption fails fundamentally: shared vegetative factors---chlorophyll absorbance, cuticle specular reflection, and leaf venation---dominate the leading eigenvectors of feature variance, masking the localized necrotic patterns that differentiate specific diseases.

To mathematically suppress this shared vegetative background and maximize the ratio of between-class to within-class scatter, we formulate an optimal linear discriminant projection. Let $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c = \frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c|}\sum_{\mathbf{z} \in \tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c} \mathbf{z}$ represent the empirical centroid of class $c$ in $\mathbb{R}^{896}$. The pooled within-class scatter matrix across all $C=11$ classes is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}_W = \frac{1}{N - C}\sum_{c=1}^C \sum_{\mathbf{z} \in \tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c} (\mathbf{z} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)(\mathbf{z} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{896 \times 896}
\end{equation}
with $N = 440$ augmented support vectors. Under this small-sample regime, the available degrees of freedom are $N - C = 429$. Because the feature dimension exceeds the sample size ($d = 896 > N = 440$), the empirical scatter matrix $\mathbf{S}_W$ is strictly rank-deficient ($\text{rank}(\mathbf{S}_W) \le 429$), possessing a null space of dimension at least $467$. Direct matrix inversion is therefore mathematically undefined.

Rather than relying on empirical diagonal loading or arbitrary heuristic jitter ($\mathbf{S}_W + \epsilon \mathbf{I}$), we resolve this singularity through analytical Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage \cite{ledoit2004well}. Ledoit--Wolf theory provides the asymptotically optimal, well-conditioned estimator under quadratic loss by finding the optimal convex combination of the sample covariance and an isotropic target:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}} = (1 - \rho^*)\mathbf{S}_W + \rho^* \nu \mathbf{I}_{896}
\end{equation}
where $\nu = \frac{1}{d}\text{Tr}(\mathbf{S}_W)$ is the average eigenvalue of the sample scatter matrix. The optimal shrinkage intensity $\rho^* \in (0, 1)$ is derived in exact closed form without requiring cross-validation:
\begin{equation}
\rho^* = \min\left(1, \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \|(\mathbf{z}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c_i})(\mathbf{z}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c_i})^T - \mathbf{S}_W\|_F^2}{N^2 \left[\|\mathbf{S}_W\|_F^2 - \frac{1}{d}\text{Tr}^2(\mathbf{S}_W)\right]}\right)
\end{equation}

Because the regularized covariance matrix $\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}$ is guaranteed to be symmetric, positive-definite, and well-conditioned, its inversion yields exact, closed-form projection parameters for multiclass classification:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{W} = \hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1} [\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_C]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times 896}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
b_c = -\frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\mu}_c^T \hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_c + \log \pi_c, \quad c \in \{1, \dots, C\}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_c$ denotes the class prior (assumed uniform, $\pi_c = 1/C$). 

At inference time, classifying a query vector $\mathbf{z}$ collapses to a single linear projection: $\mathbf{l}_{\text{fisher}} = \mathbf{z} \mathbf{W}^T + \mathbf{b}$. This requires exactly $C \times d = 11 \times 896 = 9,856$ FLOPs---a negligible computational load that executes in under $0.003$\,ms on a standard CPU. Softmax normalization yields posterior class probabilities: $\mathbf{p}_{\text{fisher}} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{l}_{\text{fisher}})$.

\subsection{Soft-Anchored Transductive Self-Calibration}
\label{sec:transductive}

While analytical Fisher alignment achieves optimal decorrelation on the support set, inductive evaluation remains vulnerable to empirical prototype shift \cite{boudiaf2020transductive}: empirical centroids estimated from only $K=5$ leaves inevitably carry finite-sample variance relative to the true underlying distribution. This shift is particularly pronounced between morphologically similar pathologies, such as \textit{Grape Esca} versus \textit{Grape Black rot}, or \textit{Potato Early} versus \textit{Late blight}.

Transductive inference mitigates this by leveraging the unlabeled marginal query distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$ to adjust the decision boundary. However, unconstrained pseudo-labeling risks confirmation bias, where confident misclassifications corrupt the manifold. To prevent this, we introduce Soft-Anchored Transductive Self-Calibration:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initial Posterior Estimation:} For an unlabelled query stream $\mathcal{Q} = \{\mathbf{x}_j\}_{j=1}^{N_q}$, we compute preliminary posteriors by ensembling visual Fisher and linguistic projections: $\mathbf{p}_j = 0.75 \mathbf{p}_{\text{fisher}, j} + 0.25 \mathbf{p}_{\text{text}, j}$, obtaining preliminary pseudo-labels $\hat{y}_j = \arg\max_c p_{j, c}$.
    \item \textbf{High-Confidence Anchor Selection:} We filter the query stream using a strict margin-entropy confidence gate ($\tau_{\text{anchor}} = 0.70$), extracting only top-confidence candidate anchors:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Q}_{\text{anchor}}^{(c)} = \text{TopK}_{20}\left(\{\mathbf{x}_j \in \mathcal{Q} \mid \hat{y}_j = c \land \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}_j) > 0.70\}\right)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Ground-Truth Support Anchoring:} To prevent noisy pseudo-labels from distorting the underlying manifold, we enforce a $3\times$ ground-truth weighting penalty on the original support set: $\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c^{\text{trans}} = (\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_c \otimes 3) \cup \mathcal{Q}_{\text{anchor}}^{(c)}$. We then analytically recompute the updated class centroids and shrunk covariance matrix $\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\text{trans}}$.
    \item \textbf{Closed-Form Projection Update:} We recompute the projection parameters $\mathbf{W}^{\text{trans}}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{\text{trans}}$ in closed form, yielding recalibrated transductive posteriors:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p}_{\text{final}}^{\text{trans}} = 0.75 \, \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{z} (\mathbf{W}^{\text{trans}})^T + \mathbf{b}^{\text{trans}}\right) + 0.25 \mathbf{p}_{\text{text}}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
This entire recalibration executes via closed-form linear algebra in under 22\,ms on an edge CPU, without invoking backpropagation or allocating GPU memory.

\textbf{Practical Streaming via Incremental Buffering.} In real-world agricultural scouting, images arrive sequentially rather than in offline evaluation batches \cite{boudiaf2020transductive}. On physical edge devices, we implement this calibration through an on-device ring buffer. Queries accumulate in memory during routine inspection; once sufficient confident anchors are gathered, $\mathbf{W}^{\text{trans}}$ updates asynchronously in a background thread ($<25$\,ms) without interrupting the live camera viewfinder. In strictly inductive single-frame mode (no query buffering), BioDINO-Edge still achieves \textbf{96.76\% on PlantVillage} and \textbf{81.79\% on PlantDoc} (Table~\ref{tab:comparative}), substantially surpassing existing baselines even without batching.

\subsection{From Mathematical Formulation to Edge Realization: Predictive Gating and Dual-Tier Deployment}
\label{sec:edge_gating}

The mathematical formulation developed above provides a crucial practical advantage: because adaptation is solved analytically and query inference collapses to inner products on frozen embeddings, the model eliminates gradient-based fine-tuning entirely. However, in real-world agricultural deployments (e.g., battery-operated field scouts and micro-drones), extracting full 896-dimensional features via both vision-language transformers on every single video frame would rapidly deplete battery reserves and throttle mobile processors.

Fortunately, our mathematical decoupling of visual geometry ($f_{\text{dino}}$) from phylogenetic priors ($f_{\text{bio}}$) allows us to construct a dynamic, confidence-gated execution pipeline: routine foliage images can be diagnosed purely via the lightweight spatial backbone, reserving the heavier multimodal companion model strictly for ambiguous cases.

\textbf{Predictive Shannon Margin-Entropy Gating.} For an incoming query image $\mathbf{x}$, the lightweight DINOv2 backbone first generates fast logits via scaled cosine similarity against the visual prototypes: $\mathbf{l}_{\text{dino}} = 20.0 \cdot (\mathbf{f}_{\text{dino}} (\mathbf{P}_{\text{dino}})^T)$, yielding preliminary class posteriors $\mathbf{p}_{\text{dino}} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{l}_{\text{dino}})$. To rigorously quantify whether this fast diagnostic path is trustworthy, we evaluate the normalized Shannon entropy:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}_{\text{norm}}(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{1}{\log C} \sum_{c=1}^C p_{\text{dino}, c} \log(p_{\text{dino}, c} + \epsilon)
\end{equation}
Because raw softmax probabilities in deep networks can exhibit overconfidence, we combine the classification margin with normalized entropy into a composite confidence metric $\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}) \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}) = \left(p_{\text{dino}, (1)} - p_{\text{dino}, (2)}\right) \cdot \left(1.0 - \mathcal{H}_{\text{norm}}(\mathbf{x})\right)_+
\end{equation}
where $p_{\text{dino}, (1)}$ and $p_{\text{dino}, (2)}$ denote the highest and second-highest predicted class probabilities, respectively. When $\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}) \ge \tau_{\text{conf}}$ (with default $\tau_{\text{conf}} = 0.35$), the diagnosis is statistically confident: inference exits immediately via the fast path in 21.1\,ms ($>$45 FPS). Only morphologically ambiguous or borderline queries ($\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}) < \tau_{\text{conf}}$) trigger the companion BioCLIP transformer and 896-d Fisher alignment.

\textbf{Dual-Tier Edge Deployment.} To accommodate rural bandwidth limits and diverse mobile hardware tiers, we translate this gated mechanism into two distinct deployment modules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Standalone Fast Path, INT8):} We compile the DINOv2 Small backbone, the fast prototype matcher, and the Shannon gating logic into an autonomous ONNX runtime graph (\texttt{biodino\_mobile\_int8\allowbreak .onnx}, 22.05\,MB working set). Packaged inside a 22.73\,MB native Android APK (15.13\,MB compressed model payload), it operates fully offline at over 45 FPS on edge CPUs.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (On-Demand Deep Companion, INT8):} We package the BioCLIP transformer, taxonomic prompts, and 896-d Fisher alignment into an optional companion binary (\texttt{biodino\_companion\_int8.onnx}, 83.57\,MB). Instead of bloating the core app, this module downloads on demand when an agronomist requires full dual-foundation verification. Total disk footprint remains capped at 105.62\,MB---comfortably within strict 110\,MB rural app delivery budgets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Benchmark Protocol}
We evaluate BioDINO-Edge on the standard PlantVillage plant pathology benchmark \cite{hughes2012plantvillage}. To test fine-grained few-shot transfer under realistic agricultural conditions, we define an 11-class intra-species challenge covering three economically important crop families:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Apple (\textit{Malus domestica}):} Apple Scab (\textit{Venturia inaequalis}), Black Rot (\textit{Botryosphaeria obtusa}), Cedar Apple Rust (\textit{Gymnosporangium juniperi-virginianae}), and Healthy foliage.
    \item \textbf{Grape (\textit{Vitis vinifera}):} Black Rot (\textit{Guignardia bidwellii}), Esca / Black Measles (\textit{Phaeomoniella chlamydospora}), Leaf Blight (\textit{Pseudocercospora vitis}), and Healthy foliage.

\end{itemize}
The split assigns exactly $K=5$ support shots to each class (55 support images in total), and the remaining $9,330$ images form the unseen query set. To avoid seed-selection bias and to give statistically sound confidence estimates, all experiments report both single-episode seed benchmarks and a 30-episode Monte Carlo evaluation with 95\% confidence intervals ($95\%\,\text{CI} = \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{30}}$). Hardware latency is measured on a standard 4-thread CPU using the compiled ONNX Runtime C++ execution engine with dynamic INT8 MatMul quantization, matching the production mobile APK environment.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset In-The-Wild Generalization on PlantDoc}
To test generalization beyond controlled laboratory foliage, we evaluate BioDINO-Edge on \textbf{PlantDoc} \cite{singh2020plantdoc}, a demanding in-the-wild benchmark of 27 shared disease and healthy categories captured under unstructured field conditions (heavy background clutter such as soil, hands, and farm equipment, as well as natural shadows and variable illumination).

We use two complementary protocols:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standard 5-way 5-shot episodic benchmark:} Following the usual few-shot practice \cite{zhou2026vsf, zhao2026lightweight}, 5 classes are sampled at random in each episode with $K=5$ support shots, and results are averaged over 50 Monte Carlo trials.
    \item \textbf{27-way all-class stress test:} A stricter, holistic evaluation in which all 27 categories are classified at once (chance level 3.70\%), using 5 support shots from the training split and testing on all 252 real-world field test queries.
\end{enumerate}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:comparative} (columns 4 and 5), under the standard 5-way 5-shot protocol BioDINO-Edge sets a new state of the art with an episodic mean accuracy of \textbf{83.61\% $\pm$ 11.69\%}. This is \textbf{13.00} points above the recent multimodal VSF model (70.61\%) and \textbf{29.37} points above Zhao \textit{et al.} (54.24\%). Individual episodes reach \textbf{100.00\%} accuracy, and the 95\% confidence ceiling is \textbf{86.85\%}. In the 27-way all-class stress test, BioDINO-Edge reaches \textbf{56.35\% accuracy} (\textbf{58.73\%} at peak) and a \textbf{55.93\% macro F1}, which is 15.2$\times$ above random guessing. It also exceeds the 5-way result of Zhao \textit{et al.} despite facing more than five times as many labels. These results suggest that combining biological taxonomic embeddings with regularized analytical covariance alignment transfers well to difficult non-foliar agricultural artifacts, without any backpropagation on the target domain.

\subsection{Explainability and Predictive Uncertainty Calibration}
Diagnostic models deployed on autonomous UAVs and field robots must be trustworthy in two respects: \textit{interpretability} (decisions must follow genuine biological lesions and not background artifacts) and \textit{calibration} (predicted confidence must reflect true empirical correctness, so that chemicals are not sprayed unnecessarily).

\textbf{Dual-regime visual explainability and attention sink suppression.}
To verify that BioDINO-Edge relies on biologically grounded foliar morphology rather than background artifacts or vegetative biases, we evaluate spatial saliency across both operational tiers (Fig.~\ref{fig:saliency}).
For Tier-1 Fast-Path queries ($\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{224 \times 224 \times 3}$), we extract the multi-head self-attention rollout $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times P \times P}$ ($H=6$ heads, $P=257$ tokens) from the final DINOv2 transformer block \cite{oquab2023dinov2}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_{\text{cls}} = \frac{1}{H} \sum_{h=1}^H \mathbf{A}_{h, 0, 1:256} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}
\end{equation}
Reshaped into a $16 \times 16$ spatial grid, $\mathbf{a}_{\text{cls}}$ is bicubically interpolated to $224 \times 224$ and normalized to $[0, 1]$ to yield the Tier-1 saliency map $\mathbf{M}_{\text{saliency}}(\mathbf{x})$.
As depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:saliency} (Columns~1--2), for routine high-confidence queries such as Apple Cedar Rust (\textit{G. juniperi-virginianae}) and Apple Black Rot (\textit{B. obtusa}), DINOv2 self-attention cleanly focuses on active fungal aecial pustules and frog-eye necrotic rings without task-specific fine-tuning. The Shannon margin-entropy metric confirms high confidence ($\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}) \ge 0.35$), granting rapid edge fast-exit in 21.1\,ms.

In contrast, Columns~3--4 demonstrate a critical structural artifact of un-fine-tuned self-supervised Vision Transformers: without register tokens, self-attention heads frequently exhibit ``attention sinks'' \cite{darcet2024vision}, wherein the global \texttt{[CLS]} token deposits residual energy into non-pathological structural boundaries, such as the petiole and primary vein junctions. In Potato Late Blight and Grape Esca, this causes raw DINOv2 attention to pool at the leaf petiole rather than the expanding necrotic margins. Because this ambiguity depresses the margin confidence below threshold ($\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}) < 0.35$), the Shannon gate reliably prevents a premature false-negative exit and routes the query to Tier~2.

In Tier~2, Regularized Fisher-LDA evaluates patch representations $\mathbf{f}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ through the discriminant projection $S_{\text{Fisher}}(p) = \mathbf{w}_c^T \mathbf{f}_p$. Because the Ledoit-Wolf precision matrix $\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_w^{-1}$ penalizes directions proportional to their intra-class foliage variance, the pervasive petiole and healthy venation directions ($\sigma_{\text{petiole}}^2 = 12.8$) are suppressed by $>31\times$, whereas the distinctive necrotic lesion morphology ($\sigma_{\text{lesion}}^2 = 0.4$) is amplified by $2.44\times$. This restores precise pathological localization onto active water-soaked blights and interveinal chlorotic necrosis, achieving calibrated consensus confidence $\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}) \ge 0.97$.

\textbf{Predictive uncertainty calibration (ECE).}
Few-shot classifiers are often miscalibrated, producing either over-confident errors or under-confident correct predictions \cite{guo2017calibration}. To quantify reliability, we compute the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and the Maximum Calibration Error (MCE) over $M=10$ equal-width confidence bins $\{B_m\}_{m=1}^M \subset (0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{N_q} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{MCE} = \max_{m \in \{1,\dots,M\}} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|
\end{equation}
where $\text{acc}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i \in B_m} \mathbf{1}(\hat{y}_i = y_i)$ and $\text{conf}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i \in B_m} \hat{p}_i$.

As Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration} (left) shows, the raw uncalibrated ensemble $\mathbf{p} = 0.75 \mathbf{p}_{\text{fisher}} + 0.25 \mathbf{p}_{\text{text}}$ is badly miscalibrated ($\text{ECE} = 37.97\%$, $\text{MCE} = 56.01\%$). The zero-shot BioCLIP text projection yields fairly diffuse posteriors across related taxa, so the linear convex blend dilutes the maximum Fisher logit. The mean confidence is then only $78.61\%$, even though test accuracy is $97.73\%$.

To correct this without altering accuracy or class ranking, we add a parameter-free post-hoc temperature scaling layer:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{\text{cal}} = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{\log(\mathbf{p} + \epsilon)}{T}\right)
\end{equation}
A single global temperature $T = 0.50$, tuned on a held-out 5-shot validation split, restores the confidence scale. As Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration} (right) shows, the calibrated BioDINO-Edge reaches \textbf{$\text{ECE} = \mathbf{0.81\%}$} and $\text{MCE} = 50.45\%$. The MCE is set by a bin that holds a single sample; among the bins with at least five samples, the largest gap is 6.09\%. The mean confidence (98.53\%) is close to the empirical test accuracy (97.73\%). A calibration error below 1\% means that downstream agricultural automation can reasonably threshold on the output confidence, for example to control automated chemical dispensing.

\section{Discussion and Practical Deployment}
Deploying vision AI in real agricultural environments means balancing diagnostic accuracy, hardware latency, and operational autonomy.

\textbf{Trustworthy robotic scouting.} By combining normalized Shannon entropy with margin confidence, BioDINO-Edge quantifies diagnostic uncertainty on every frame. On autonomous agricultural UAVs, high-uncertainty frames can trigger a localized re-inspection or alert a human agronomist, which helps prevent erroneous fungicide application.

\textbf{The host-context loss dilemma (lesion cropping ablation).} A common intuition in agricultural vision is that tight bounding boxes around lesions remove non-foliar field clutter such as soil, hands, and weeds. We tested this by using DINOv2 self-attention rollout to crop the main lesion clusters automatically across all 27 PlantDoc categories. Contrary to expectation, 27-way accuracy dropped slightly from 58.73\% to 57.14\% (macro F1: 57.95\% $\to$ 56.79\%). A detailed error analysis showed that tight crops remove the macroscopic host morphology (compound versus simple leaflet layout, venation symmetry), which causes inter-species confusion between homologous necrotic spots on different crops (for example, Early Blight on tomato versus potato). This points to a useful design lesson: for fine-grained taxonomic few-shot grounding, keeping the macroscopic host context matters as much as isolating the lesion.

\textbf{Zero-gradient, training-free adaptation.} Meta-learning and fine-tuning approaches need backpropagation and GPU compute. In our method, both the Fisher manifold alignment and the transductive self-calibration are closed-form. Edge devices can therefore adapt to newly emerging pathogens within milliseconds, without draining the battery or triggering thermal throttling.

\textbf{Dual-tier practicality for rural farms.} Rural farming regions often have intermittent or low-bandwidth internet access. The dual-tier design keeps the core application compact (a 22.73\,MB standalone APK packaging the 15.13\,MB losslessly compressed model), so diagnosis works offline from the first launch. The deep companion module can be downloaded later, when a high-bandwidth connection is available.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented \textbf{BioDINO-Edge}, an adaptive dual-foundation framework that unites self-supervised visual patch modeling (DINOv2 Small) with organismal taxonomic vision-language supervision (BioCLIP ViT-B). By combining high-dimensional Regularized Fisher-Discriminant Alignment with closed-form Soft-Anchored Transductive Self-Calibration and predictive Shannon uncertainty gating, it sets a new benchmark for few-shot plant pathology: \textbf{98.72\% peak accuracy}, \textbf{98.41\% peak F1-score}, and a 30-episode mean of \textbf{97.37\% $\pm$ 0.73\%} on the 11-class PlantVillage 5-shot benchmark, with false-negative disease errors held to \textbf{0.604\%} and cross-crop confusion to \textbf{0.011\%}. The system fits in a very small edge footprint (a 22.73\,MB standalone APK with a 15.13\,MB losslessly compressed fast path, plus an 83.6\,MB on-demand companion) and delivers real-time throughput above 45 FPS on edge CPUs. This makes it a trustworthy, field-ready solution for automated crop health scouting.
