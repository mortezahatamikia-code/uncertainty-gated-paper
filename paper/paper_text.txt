1. Introduction
Crop pests and foliar diseases destroy an estimated 20% to 40% of global
agricultural yield annually, incurring over $220 billion in direct economic
losses and severely jeopardizing food security for vulnerable populations [1].
In conventional agronomic management, disease identication relies heavily
on manual scouting and visual inspection by trained phytopathologists [2, 3].
However, manual scouting is labor-intensive, error-prone under eld fatigue,
geographically unscalable across vast acreages, and susceptible to misdiagnosis when distinct pathogens produce visually homologous symptoms [3].
Deep learning and convolutional architectures have introduced automated
alternatives by extracting discriminative lesion representations directly from
redgreenblue (RGB) imagery [3].

Nonetheless, conventional supervised

paradigms require thousands of curated, expert-annotated images per pathology to train generalized classiers [4]. In operational agriculture, such massive datasets are impractical: novel pathogen strains and physiological mutations emerge abruptly with seasonal shifts, while expert botanical labeling
remains prohibitively expensive [5]. Consequently, Few-Shot Learning (FSL)
has emerged as an indispensable paradigm: adapting frozen visual backbones
to novel disease categories given only a handful (K = 5) of support leaf samples [6, 5].
Recent agricultural FSL literature has explored cross-domain meta-learning
architectures such as CDFSL-BDC (cross-domain few-shot learning with
Brownian distance covariance) [7] and MAFDE-DN4 (multi-scale attention
fusion and discriminative enhancement over a deep nearest-neighbor network)

2

[8], alongside few-shot metric domain adaptation (FMDA) [9], lightweight
networks such as AgriFewNet [10], visual-semantic fusion (VSF) [11], and
edge-federated frameworks [12]. Despite notable progress, four fundamental
technical bottlenecks persist in real-world agricultural deployments:
1.

Vegetative Manifold Dominance: Over 90% of an infected leaf 's
visual area comprises healthy green chlorophyll, venation, and natural illumination, while pathological lesions occupy less than 5%10%
of the surface [3]. In standard feature spaces, isotropic distance metrics (e.g., Euclidean or cosine similarity) are overwhelmed by shared
foliar variance, causing severe cross-disease confusion between distinct
pathologies of the same host species (e.g., Grape Black Rot vs. Grape
Esca) [11].

High-Dimensional Covariance Singularity (N ≪ d): Discrimi-

and analytically non-invertible [14].

The Edge Adaptation Paradox:

While parameter-ecient ne-

Raspberry Pi 5, mobile phones, or agricultural scouting drones) [16].
4.

Memory & Rural Connectivity Constraints: Modern foundation

models typically exceed hundreds of megabytes or gigabytes, preventing
packaging into lightweight standalone mobile applications suitable for
rural areas lacking broadband or cellular connectivity [16, 12].
To overcome these foundational limitations, we introduce

2. Related Work
Few-shot learning (FSL) adapts a model to novel classes from a handful of
labeled samples. Metric-based methods classify queries by distance to class
prototypes or support embeddings [6, 21], while optimization-based methods
learn an initialization that adapts rapidly [22]; training-free adapters such
as Tip-Adapter cache support features to augment a frozen visionlanguage
model [23], and prompt-learning approaches adapt the textual side of CLIPstyle models [24].

Transductive FSL instead exploits the unlabeled query

set, either by information maximization and entropy regularization [25, 26],
Laplacian label propagation [27], prototype renement with optimal transport [28], or adaptive context networks [29]. These methods typically iterate
over a query graph or optimize logits at inference time, which is attractive
on servers but costly under on-device latency and memory budgets.
Vision foundation models provide strong frozen representations:

CLIP

couples images and text through contrastive pretraining [30], DINOv2 learns
robust self-supervised patch features [17], and BioCLIP specializes the vision
language space to organismal taxonomy [19]. In plant pathology, FSL has
been approached with cross-domain meta-learning [7], multi-scale attention
and discriminative enhancement [8], few-shot metric domain adaptation [9],
lightweight networks [10], visualsemantic fusion [11], and edge-federated
frameworks [12]; recent surveys review the broader landscape [31, 32]. Parameterecient ne-tuning such as LoRA improves adaptation accuracy [15], but
requires on-device backpropagation or GPU ooading, which conicts with
the thermal and hardware constraints of rural edge hardware [16].
Our work diers in three respects. First, it couples two complementary
frozen foundation modelsspatial DINOv2 patches and taxonomic BioCLIP
embeddingsinto a single 896-D descriptor without any ne-tuning. Second,
it replaces iterative transductive optimization with a closed-form Ledoit
Wolf-regularized Fisher projection, so Phase-0 calibration and background
recalibration cost sub-second algebra on a mobile CPU. Third, it packages
the resulting cascade as a standalone oine Android application and evaluates routing, calibration, and selective risk on a physical device.

The shrinkage analysis of Section 3.4.3 assumes i.i.d. support samples,

We quantify the consequences with a one-factor-at-a-time study over the

photometric-only color jitter vs. the full pipeline) over the rst 10 episodes;
and (iii) a pure-replication control that tiles the canonical embedding eight
times (N = 440, zero within-image diversity). For each condition we report
the mean per-class LedoitWolf intensity α̂, the condition number κ(Σ̂) of
the pooled within-class covariance, and downstream accuracy (Table 9).

vs. 97.41%; Appendix Appendix A), conrming that the study isolates the
augmentation factor alone.

5.6. Predictive Uncertainty, Calibration, and Routing Reliability
In safety-critical agricultural automation such as robotic spraying, an
overcondent misdiagnosis triggers an unwarranted chemical application, so
the routing gate needs a direct reliability audit rather than a single calibration score; calibration is known to depend on architecture and training
recipe, not on accuracy alone [38]. Temperature is tted on an independent
stratied 50% split of the 9,330-query PlantVillage stream (4,662 calibration
/ 4,668 evaluation queries), minimizing negative log-likelihood on the calibration split only [39]. Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Maximum
Calibration Error (MCE) use M = 10 equal-width condence bins; to expose
small-bin artifacts we also report equal-mass (adaptive) binning and MCE

30

over bins with n ≥ 30. The deployed gate consumes the uncalibrated Tier-1
posterior: temperature scaling is a post-hoc audit applied after routing and
never changes the gate score or the threshold.

Tier-1 calibration. The raw gate probabilities are under-condent (full-

stream ECE 32.07%, MCE 38.07%; held-out ECE 31.76%).

A scalar logit

temperature tted on the calibration split (T = 0.267) reduces held-out ECE
to 1.20% (MCE 16.71%; Fig. 3ab). Routing itself uses the raw scores, so we
verify the gate directly below rather than relying on ECE.

Tier-2 calibration. The rened Tier-2 consensus is also under-condent

(ECE 19.45%), and most of the error sits in the dominant bin (0.7, 0.8]: 7,102
queries at 98.0% accuracy but 77.5% mean condence (Table 10). The equalwidth MCE of 44.26% comes from bins with at most nine samples (one query
in (0.4, 0.5]); equal-mass binning lowers it to 21.53%, and bins with n ≥ 30
give 20.49%.

Scalar logit temperature scaling cannot remove this residual:

the consensus is a xed 0.75/0.25 blend of Fisher logits and BioCLIP text
probabilities, so softening the logits alone leaves the blend miscalibrated (the
tted T hits the optimizer bound and held-out ECE moves 19.33%→19.83%).
A power transform softmax(log p/T ) with T = 0.50 instead collapses nearly
all mass into one or two bins, producing a small ECE (0.48%) but an equalwidth MCE of 80.85% (Fig. 3d); a freshly tted power temperature (T

=
0.54) reproduces this (held-out ECE 0.24%, MCE 88.74%). The large equal-

width MCE therefore reects a single-sample bin rather than widespread
miscalibration.

We therefore do not claim that Tier-2 is well calibrated;

routing reliability is instead veried through tier-separated error rates and
selective risk.

6. Engineering Applications, Practical Deployment, and Limitations
6.1. Practical Deployment on Agricultural IoT and Mobile Hardware
BioDINO-Edge is engineered specically for practical agronomic deployment on power-constrained edge hardware such as handheld scouting devices,

37

smart tractor booms, and autonomous unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs).
Eliminating gradient backpropagation avoids GPU memory spikes and the
heat of training-time optimisation: the processor executes purely feed-forward
inference and closed-form linear algebra. Sustained CPU inference still thermally throttlesa 600 s soak settles to 4.26 FPS at thermal status 3 versus
a 16.7 FPS cool-start burst (Section 5.7)so the system targets interactive
per-frame scouting rather than continuous video.

The two-tier packaging

allows agricultural cooperatives to deploy the ≈22.8 MB base APK over cellular networks, while downloading the companion module only when deep
multimodal escalation is needed.

6.2. Limitations and Future Research Directions
BioDINO-Edge processes images at the individual leaf frame level.

In

unsegmented eld imagery with dense, overlapping canopies, multiple leaves
from neighboring plants can enter the camera eld, lowering gating condence. While tight lesion cropping degrades host context, processing unsegmented canopy clutter introduces irrelevant background vectors. A promising future direction is hierarchical spatial parsing: rst segmenting individual foliar targets from canopy clutter, then performing closed-form discriminant diagnosis on the isolated targets.

A further statistical limitation is

that the multi-view support expansion produces correlated pseudo-replicates:
the analytical LedoitWolf intensity assumes i.i.d. sampling, so the nominal

N − C = 429 degrees of freedom overstate the independent information content of the support set (eective sample size between 55 and 440).

The

sensitivity study in Section 5.5 conrms that pure replication deates the
estimated intensity by ≈8× without adding information, yet downstream accuracy remains stable across view counts and augmentation types; a clusteraware shrinkage estimator that explicitly discounts within-image dependence
remains future work.

Finally, the frozen encoders' self-attention exhibits

sinks on non-lesion structures such as veins and petioles [42]; because the
Fisher-LDA operates on pooled embeddings downstream, it does not modify encoder attention, and the framework makes no lesion-localization or
attention-modication claim.
A further practical limitation concerns degraded inputs. Severe defocus
blur attenuates the high-frequency lesion textures on which the frozen encoders rely and degrades diagnostic accuracy. The deployed Android client
rejects clearly invalid frames: when the maximum cosine similarity between
the query embedding and the leaf class prototypes falls below a xed foliage

38

threshold, or when mean luminance indicates a dark scene or covered lens,
the frame receives zero condence and the operator is prompted to re-aim
the camera; a center-crop Laplacian-variance check additionally warns the
operator to hold the camera steady under blur. This blur check only warns
and does not abstain, however, so severely defocused frames can still receive a
diagnosis. The uncertainty gate itself is a router, not a rejector: ambiguous
queries escalate to Tier-2 rather than being refused, and abstention exists
only in the optional selective mode (Table 12). Extending the quality guard
to blur-aware abstention with a refocus prompt, together with systematic
evaluation of error detection on out-of-distribution and low-quality eld imagery, remains future work.

Scope and threats to validity. The edge measurements come from

a single physical device (Samsung Galaxy S24 FE) with CPU-only INT8
ONNX Runtime; absolute latency and memory will dier on other SoCs, although the algorithmic cost and the delegate-agnostic pipeline do not. The
controlled comparison (Table 2) isolates the adaptation algorithm on identical frozen features and re-runs the training-free inductive baselines; iterative transductive FSL methods (Section 2) are referenced but not re-run,
so the reported transductive gain is measured against our own inductive
mode rather than against those methods.

The PlantDoc protocol uses la-

beled target-domain support; a true source→target cross-domain transfer
(e.g., PlantVillage→PlantDoc), quantitative image-quality stress tests, and
multi-device proling remain future work.

7. Conclusion
In this paper, we presented

BioDINO-Edge, a gradient-free few-shot fo-

liar disease diagnostic framework that adapts frozen DINOv2 and BioCLIP
foundation models without backpropagation. By resolving small-sample covariance singularity (N

≪ d) through closed-form LedoitWolf shrinkage,

BioDINO-Edge down-weights dominant shared vegetative variance directions
via inverse-variance whitening, computing optimal Fisher-LDA discriminant
boundaries in ≈0.53 s of gradient-free closed-form algebra on a mobile edge
CPU. An adaptive condence gate combining normalized Shannon entropy
and top-2 margin resolves routine foliage in 59.78 ms (≈16.7 FPS), escalating ambiguous queries to a multimodal classier with soft-anchored transductive recalibration. Packaged into a standalone ≈22.8 MB Android APK,
BioDINO-Edge achieves 97.41% accuracy on 11-class PlantVillage with a

39

0.702% false-negative rate and 83.61% on in-the-wild PlantDoc, oering an
accurate, interactive diagnostic solution for resource-constrained agricultural
edge platforms.
